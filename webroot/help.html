1 · what this module does

The module blocks traffic by IP address, using the Linux kernel's ipset together with iptables (IPv4) and ip6tables (IPv6). Lists of known-bad networks — botnets, malware control servers, hijacked address space — are loaded into the kernel, and every connection to or from one of them is stopped.

DNS blocking (like the DNSCrypt module) stops a name from being looked up. It cannot stop an app that connects to a hard-coded IP address without any lookup. Blocking by address does. The two work well together.

requirement
CPUarm64 only
RootSukiSU Ultra, KernelSU or APatch (WebUI built in). Magisk works too — open the WebUI with MMRL or KSU WebUI Standalone.
KernelCONFIG_IP_SET and CONFIG_NETFILTER_XT_SET. System → Device shows whether your kernel has them. Most GKI kernels do.
IPv6Filtered too when the device has ip6tables with the set match. If not, IPv4 still works and the Dashboard says so.
2 · quick start
  1. Flash the zip in your root manager and reboot.
  2. Open the module's WebUI. The banner says No blocklists downloaded yet — a fresh install downloads nothing on its own.
  3. Go to Lists. FireHOL Level 1 and Spamhaus DROPv6 are already ticked (the recommended pair). Change the selection if you like and tap Save selection.
  4. Tap Update lists and watch the log below the button.
  5. Back on the Dashboard the banner turns to Protected.
  6. Optional: under Automatic update choose Daily or Weekly to keep the lists fresh.
Try it: Tools → Check an address tells you whether an address is on a list; Test connection actually connects to it and tells you whether this module stopped it.
3 · how blocking works

The module keeps its rules in its own chains, linked at the top of Android's OUTPUT, INPUT and FORWARD chains. Every packet takes this path, and the first match decides. Stop means refuse at once or drop silently, as set in System.

outgoing phone → internet 1 loopback pass 2 your adv. rules as written 3 your allowlist pass 4 DNSCrypt resolvers pass 5 LAN, special pass 6 Outgoing/Both lists stop incoming internet → phone 1 loopback pass 2 your adv. rules as written 3 replies to the phone's own conns pass 4 allowlist, DNSCrypt, LAN, special pass 5 Incoming/Both lists drop

What is never blocked

Loopback, private LAN ranges (10/8, 172.16/12, 192.168/16), carrier NAT (100.64/10), link-local, multicast and the other special-purpose ranges. Some lists include them because they were made for routers at the edge of a network; on a phone they would cut you off from your own Wi-Fi router. The module removes every list entry that overlaps them.

Safe by design

  • No gaps while updating. A new list is built next to the live one and swapped in at once. The firewall rules are replaced in a single step too, and are checked by the kernel before they are installed.
  • A list never shrinks by accident. A download that fails, or brings far fewer entries than expected, is thrown away and the last good copy stays in use.
  • The watchdog checks every 10 seconds that the rules are still in place. Android's network daemon wipes the links to the module's chains when it restarts; the watchdog puts them back within seconds. The Dashboard counts these repairs.
  • If something fails, the network keeps working. A rule the kernel refuses is left out and reported; it never takes the phone offline.
4 · dashboard

The banner at the top is the answer to "am I protected right now?":

bannermeaning
ProtectedThe rules are active, the lists are loaded, on IPv4 and IPv6 (or IPv4 only, if IPv6 filtering is switched off).
No listsThe rules are in place but no list is downloaded yet, so nothing is being blocked. Open Lists and tap Update.
IPv4 onlyIPv6 cannot be filtered on this device (see System for the reason). IPv6 traffic passes unchecked.
PausedYou paused protection; it comes back by itself when the time is up.
Switched offThe Protection switch is off. Nothing is blocked until you switch it on.
Not blockingThe IPv4 rules could not be installed. See System and the Log.

Numbers

Networks is the number of networks on all active lists. Blocked out and Blocked in count packets stopped since the rules were last applied — they start again from zero after a reboot and after settings that rebuild the rules. Lists shows loaded / selected.

Pause

15 minutes, 1 hour or 4 hours. The rules are unlinked and relinked by themselves when the time is up. A reboot also ends a pause. The lists stay loaded while paused.

5 · lists — the blocklists
listsizewhat it holds
FireHOL Level 1~4,700Recommended. Botnets, malware control servers, hijacked networks — chosen for a minimum of false positives.
FireHOL Level 2~18,500Attacks seen in the last 48 hours. Some false positives possible.
FireHOL Level 3~12,000Attackers, spyware and malware seen in the last 30 days. More false positives.
FireHOL Level 4~160,000Aggressive and very large. Expect some sites or services to break.
FireHOL Web client~470A small list of addresses browsers and apps should never talk to.
Spamhaus DROPv6~90Recommended. IPv6 networks hijacked or operated by professional cyber-crime.

Sizes change with every update. The FireHOL lists are IPv4 only; public IPv6 blocklists are rare, and Spamhaus DROPv6 is the one used here.

Choosing

Tick the lists and tap Save selection. Lists that were downloaded before are loaded from their copy at once; lists that were never downloaded are fetched. Unticking a list takes it out of the kernel but keeps its copy, so ticking it again is instant.

View

View opens the networks of a downloaded list, with a search box (type part of an address, e.g. 103.117). From there:

  • Let through — puts that network on your allowlist. A list is replaced completely on every update, so a line deleted from it would simply come back; the allowlist always wins and survives updates.
  • Block one more — adds an address or network to your own blocklist.
6 · lists — outgoing, incoming, both

Each list, and your own blocklist, has its own direction:

directioneffect
OutgoingConnections from this phone to a listed address are stopped. Something on that address can still reach the phone.
IncomingConnections a listed address starts towards this phone are dropped. Connections this phone opens to it still work — their replies are let through.
BothBoth of the above. The default.

Example: FireHOL Level 1 on Both, and the much larger Level 4 on Incoming only — scanners and attackers cannot reach the phone, but no site you visit breaks because of Level 4's false positives.

A phone behind mobile data or home Wi-Fi rarely receives unsolicited connections from the internet — the carrier's NAT or the router stops most of them. Outgoing (or Both) is where a list does most of its work.

Changing a direction takes effect at once, without rebuilding the rules, so the Blocked counters keep counting.

7 · lists — updating

Update lists downloads every selected list in the background. The log under the button shows each list as it arrives. The rest of the WebUI keeps working meanwhile.

Automatic update

settingbehaviour
OffThe default. Nothing is ever downloaded unless you tap Update or select a list that has never been downloaded.
DailyEach selected list is refreshed once it is a day old.
WeeklyEach selected list is refreshed once it is a week old.

The Dashboard shows when the next automatic update is due (next ≈ tomorrow 17:13) and how the last one went — manual or automatic, when, and which lists failed. A failed update turns the row red and puts a red dot on the Lists tab; the lists you had stay in use. The result is kept across reboots.

The watchdog checks once a minute, starting two minutes after boot (when the network is usually up). Ages are measured with the real clock only after it has been set. A failed download is retried later — never more often than once an hour per list — and the last good copy stays in use in the meantime. Spamhaus asks for downloads at most once an hour; the module keeps to that.

Download tools

Downloads use curl if the system has it, otherwise busybox wget (included with SukiSU, KernelSU and APatch), otherwise toybox wget.

8 · tools — check & test an address

Check

Looks an address up in every active list, your own lists and the special ranges, and tells you what the module would do with it. Nothing is sent over the network.

verdictmeaning
BlockedOn a list set to Both; both directions are stopped. The lists holding it are named.
Blocked outgoingOnly on lists set to Outgoing.
Blocked incomingOnly on lists set to Incoming.
AllowedOn your allowlist, which wins over every list.
Never blockedA LAN or special-purpose address, or one the DNSCrypt module needs.
Not listedNo active list holds it; its traffic passes.

Test connection

Actually connects from the phone to the address — like curl in a terminal — on the port you give (443, HTTPS, by default; 80 is HTTP; any port works). Whether this module stopped it is read from its own rule counters, so the answer does not rely on guessing from the timing:

resultmeaning
ConnectedThe connection went through.
Stopped by this moduleRefused at once (Refuse setting) or dropped (Drop setting) by one of the module's rules. It says whether the outgoing or the incoming rule did it.
Refused elsewhereRefused, but not by this module — usually nothing listens on that port there. Try 443 or 80.
No answerNothing came back in 4 seconds, and not because of this module: the host is down, the port is filtered, or there is no route.

The test runs as root, so advanced rules limited to one app do not apply to it.

9 · tools — your blocklist & allowlist

One address or network per entry, IPv4 or IPv6, for example 203.0.113.7, 198.51.100.0/24, 2001:db8::/32. Several can be added at once, separated by spaces or commas.

  • Your blocklist works like one more list, with its own direction (Outgoing / Incoming / Both).
  • Your allowlist always wins: an address on it is never blocked by any list, in either direction. Use it for a site a list blocks by mistake.

Both are plain text files, also kept in /sdcard/ipset-arm64/ — ip-blocklist.txt and ip-allowlist.txt. Edit them with any app; the watchdog applies your changes within about 10 seconds. Lines starting with # are comments; invalid lines are skipped and counted in the log.

10 · tools — advanced sets & rules

For experts: your own ipset sets and your own firewall rules for them, as in the earlier versions of this module.

Sets

Create a set with a name (letters, digits, underscore, up to 31 characters), a type and a family. hash:net holds addresses and networks; hash:ip,port holds entries like 1.2.3.4,tcp:443; hash:net,iface holds 10.0.0.0/8,wlan0. Tap a set to see, add and remove its members. The kernel decides which types it supports.

Rules

fieldchoices
TrafficOutgoing, Incoming, or Hotspot (devices using this phone's hotspot or USB tethering).
Matchdst = where the packet goes, src = where it comes from. Outgoing traffic is usually matched by dst, incoming by src; the form switches for you. Two-dimensional sets such as hash:ip,port use dst,dst.
ActionDROP and REJECT stop the packet; ACCEPT lets it through past every list; RETURN means "no decision here" and the packet goes on to the lists.
AppOptional, outgoing only: search an installed app by name and the rule applies to that app alone.

Your rules run before the allowlist and the lists, exactly as written, in the order you added them — on IPv4 or IPv6 depending on the set's family. A rule the kernel refuses (a set type and match that do not fit together) is not saved and you get the reason.

Deleting a set that rules still use asks whether to delete those rules too. Sets named ipsa_… belong to the module and cannot be created or changed here. Sets created by other apps are listed but never touched.

11 · system

Health

Whether the IPv4 and IPv6 rules are active (and why not, if not), whether they are linked into Android's chains, the watchdog and its repairs, and which lists are in the kernel.

Settings

settingeffect
Filter IPv6Apply the lists to IPv6 as well. On by default.
Filter hotspot clientsApply the lists (their outgoing direction) to devices using this phone's hotspot or USB tethering. Off by default.
Protect DNSCrypt resolversNever block the addresses the DNSCrypt module uses — see section 15. On by default.
Refuse at once / Drop silentlyWhat happens to an outgoing connection to a blocked address. Refuse: the app gets an error immediately (TCP is answered with a reset, the rest with an ICMP error). Drop: the packet vanishes and the app waits for its own timeout. Incoming packets from blocked addresses are always dropped.

Re-apply rules

Rebuilds the module's chains from the settings, your rules and the lists. Normally not needed — the watchdog does this by itself whenever something changes.

15 · dnscrypt, vpn & other firewalls

DNSCrypt module

If dnscrypt-proxy-android-arm64-only is installed, this module reads its configuration and never blocks the addresses it depends on: its bootstrap resolvers, its connectivity probe address and the servers pinned in its [static] section. Otherwise a list that happened to contain one of them would take DNS away from the whole phone. When you switch resolvers in the DNSCrypt WebUI, this module follows within a minute. System shows how many addresses are protected; the switch there turns this off.

A resolver reached by hostname only (a DoH server without an address in its stamp) has no fixed address to protect; its address is looked up through the bootstrap resolvers, which are protected.

VPN

Apps' traffic still passes the module's rules with its real destination before it enters the VPN tunnel, so blocking keeps working with a VPN on. The VPN server's own address is only blocked if a list contains it; if that happens, put it on your allowlist.

Other firewalls

The module only adds its own chains and three links at the top of OUTPUT, INPUT and FORWARD. It does not change the rules of AFWall+ or other firewall apps. If another app removes the module's links, the watchdog puts them back.

16 · troubleshooting

A site or app stopped working

Find the address it connects to (the app's error, or a network log), then Tools → Check. If a list blocks it, Allow it. To confirm, Test connection should now say Connected. If Level 3 or 4 is on, try turning it off or setting it to Incoming.

"IPv4 only" / IPv6 not filtered

System → Health gives the reason. unable to initialize table 'filter' means the kernel has no IPv6 firewall. IPv4 protection is unaffected.

Lists do not download

Lists → the log under Update shows why, and Log → Last update keeps it. Common causes: no connection yet, a captive Wi-Fi portal, or the list site being blocked by another app. The last good copy stays in use.

An advanced rule is not active

System → Health says if a rule is missing its set or was refused by the kernel. The module log names the rule.

Watchdog stopped

Reboot. If it stays stopped, the first lines of Log → Last boot say what is missing (usually the ipset binary or iptables-restore).

Everything off, right now

Dashboard → switch Protection off, or from a shell: sh /data/adb/modules/ipset_arm64/ctl.sh disable. Disabling the module in the root manager and rebooting also removes all its rules.

17 · update & uninstall

Updating keeps everything in /data/adb/ipset_arm64_data/: settings, your lists, downloaded lists, your advanced sets and rules. New settings are added to settings.conf with their defaults; your values are never changed. Reboot after flashing.

Uninstalling from the root manager removes every chain, link and set of the module, your advanced sets and rules, and /data/adb/ipset_arm64_data/. Sets and rules that belong to other apps are left alone. The copies of your lists in /sdcard/ipset-arm64/ are yours and stay.
